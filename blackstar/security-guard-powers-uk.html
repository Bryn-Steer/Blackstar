What Can Security Guards Legally Do in the UK?

A clear guide to security guard powers in the UK, including arrest, detention, searches, reasonable force, removing people from premises and what an SIA licence actually allows.

Security Guard Powers

The Short Answer

Security guards are not police officers. Holding an SIA licence does not give a private security operative the general powers of arrest, detention, stop and search available to the police.

The Security Industry Authority states that security operatives do not have special legal powers above those available to other members of the public.

That does not mean security guards are powerless. Depending on the circumstances, they may be able to refuse entry, ask someone to leave private premises, carry out consensual searches, make a lawful citizen's arrest and use reasonable force where the law permits it.

The important distinction is that each action needs its own lawful basis. A uniform or SIA licence does not create a general power to search, restrain or arrest somebody.

The First Distinction

Do Security Guards Have the Same Powers as Police?

No. An SIA licensed security guard is not a police officer and does not receive police powers simply because they work in private security.

The SIA has expressly stated that licensed security operatives have no special legal powers over and above other civilians.

Police officers have specific statutory powers relating to arrest, detention, stop and search and investigation. A security guard must instead rely on the law applying to ordinary citizens, together with the authority they have when acting on behalf of a property owner or occupier.

Arrest & Detention

Can Security Guards Detain You in the UK?

A security guard does not have a general power to detain somebody simply because they appear suspicious, refuse to answer questions or make the guard uncomfortable.

In England and Wales, a security operative may in limited circumstances make the same citizen's arrest available to another member of the public under section 24A of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984.

Where a lawful citizen's arrest has been made, the person may necessarily be prevented from leaving while responsibility is transferred to the police.

Detention Is Not a Standalone Security Power

Holding somebody without a lawful basis can expose the operative and security provider to serious legal consequences. The fact that somebody is an SIA licence holder does not by itself authorise detention.

Section 24A PACE

Can a Security Guard Arrest You?

In England and Wales, yes, but only under the limited arrest power that can also be exercised by another member of the public.

Section 24A of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 applies to indictable offences and places conditions on when an arrest by somebody other than a police constable may be made.

The arrest must fall within the statutory conditions

  • The person is committing or is reasonably suspected of committing an indictable offence
  • Or an indictable offence has been committed and the person is guilty of it or reasonably suspected of being guilty of it
  • The arrest is necessary to prevent physical injury
  • Or to prevent loss of or damage to property
  • Or to prevent the person making off before a constable can take responsibility
  • It is not reasonably practicable for a police constable to make the arrest instead

Because these conditions are restrictive, professional security teams should treat citizen's arrest as a serious intervention rather than a routine power.

Physical Intervention

Can Security Guards Use Reasonable Force?

Security guards can potentially use physical force, but there must be a lawful reason for doing so.

In England and Wales, section 3 of the Criminal Law Act 1967 permits reasonable force in the prevention of crime or in effecting or assisting a lawful arrest.

Common law also recognises reasonable force in self-defence and the defence of another person.

Force Must Still Be Justified

The SIA's current guidance emphasises that force needs to be necessary in the circumstances and proportionate to the threat. An SIA licence is not permission to use force whenever somebody refuses to cooperate.

Physical Contact

Can a Security Guard Touch You?

There is no general right for a security guard to grab, restrain or physically handle somebody simply because they work in security.

Physical contact can nevertheless be lawful where there is a recognised legal basis for it, such as reasonable self-defence, defence of another person, prevention of crime, a lawful arrest or the lawful removal of somebody from private premises.

The level of physical intervention should be determined by the circumstances. Good professional security places communication and de-escalation before physical intervention wherever that is safely possible.

Search Powers

Can Security Guards Search You in the UK?

Private security guards do not receive the general statutory stop-and-search powers of police officers.

However, a venue, event or private premises can make agreement to a security search a condition of entry. In those circumstances, the search is based on consent.

A person can normally refuse to consent to an entry search, but the venue may then refuse them admission in accordance with its lawful entry policy.

Security Search vs Police Stop and Search

These should not be confused. A consent-based venue search is not the same legal power as a police officer exercising statutory stop-and-search powers.

Bags & Property

Can a Security Guard Search Your Bag?

A bag can be searched where the individual has consented to the search.

In March 2026, the SIA specifically confirmed that an SIA licensed door supervisor may put their hands into an individual's bag when conducting a search, provided that the individual has consented.

The operative should also follow the relevant assignment instructions set by the security provider or venue, which may specify how searches are carried out and whether equipment such as a search wand is used.

Private Premises

Can Security Guards Refuse You Entry?

Security personnel acting on behalf of a venue or property occupier can enforce lawful conditions of entry.

SIA door-supervision training specifically covers refusing entry in circumstances including capacity, intoxication, age restrictions, refusal to be searched, possession of prohibited items and breaches of venue rules.

That authority does not remove the venue's obligation to comply with other applicable law, including rules against unlawful discrimination.

Removing People

Can Security Guards Remove You from Private Premises?

A venue can withdraw somebody's permission to remain on private premises and ask them to leave.

SIA guidance on removing trespassers recognises that a person who refuses to leave after being asked can be lawfully ejected, with physical force limited to what is lawfully justified in the circumstances.

SIA door-supervisor training places physical intervention after communication and conflict-management techniques and treats it as a last resort.

This is particularly important in bars, nightclubs, events and other customer-facing environments where a badly managed ejection can create additional risk rather than resolve it.

Restraints

Can Security Guards Use Handcuffs in the UK?

An SIA licence does not give a security guard a special statutory power to handcuff somebody.

The SIA states that UK law does not generally prohibit a civilian from carrying handcuffs, but improper use can amount to a criminal or civil wrong.

Where restraints are used, the operative would need to be able to justify why their use was lawful, reasonable and proportionate in the circumstances. The SIA does not endorse or encourage their use simply because somebody holds a security licence.

Retail Security

Can a Security Guard Stop or Detain a Shoplifter?

Retail security does not receive a special shoplifting arrest power simply because the operative is employed as a security guard.

In England and Wales, the relevant legal route may be the same section 24A citizen's-arrest power available to another member of the public, provided all of its conditions are met.

The SIA has specifically confirmed that an operative may use reasonable force where lawful in the prevention of crime or when carrying out a lawful citizen's arrest.

Security providers should therefore have clear assignment instructions explaining how retail staff are expected to respond to suspected theft rather than encouraging indiscriminate physical intervention.

Know the Limits

What Can't Security Guards Legally Do?

The exact legality of an incident always depends on the circumstances, but an SIA licence does not give security personnel a general right to:

  • Exercise the general stop-and-search powers available to police
  • Search somebody simply because the guard suspects them of wrongdoing
  • Detain somebody without a lawful basis
  • Use unlimited force because somebody refuses to cooperate
  • Make an arrest as though they were a police constable
  • Treat an SIA licence as authority to act outside ordinary law
SIA Licensing

What Does an SIA Licence Actually Allow a Security Guard to Do?

An SIA licence should be understood as permission to perform particular licensable private security activities, not as a grant of police powers.

For example, an SIA Security Guard licence covers relevant security guarding activity involving the protection of premises or property against damage, theft, unauthorised access or disorderly behaviour.

Door supervision has a different licence scope. An appropriately licensed door supervisor can carry out relevant guarding activity at licensed premises and the licence also permits security-guarding activity.

Important Legal Context

Do Security Guard Powers Work the Same Across the Whole UK?

No. The SIA regulates private-security licensing across the UK, but criminal law and arrest powers are not identical in England and Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Because Blackstar operates in Manchester, the detailed citizen's-arrest explanation in this guide focuses primarily on England and Wales.

Northern Ireland has an equivalent statutory provision for an arrest by somebody other than a constable, while Scotland operates under a different legal framework. Specific incidents should therefore be assessed under the law applying in the relevant jurisdiction.

Professional Security

Why Understanding Security Guard Powers Matters to Businesses

Businesses are not simply hiring a uniform. They are trusting security personnel to make decisions involving customers, staff, property and potentially difficult situations.

A professional security provider should therefore understand the boundaries of the role, brief staff properly and make clear when to communicate, when to de-escalate, when to contact police and when physical intervention is legally justified.

Good security is not about exercising the maximum possible power. It is about making the right decision at the right moment and using the least intrusive response that safely achieves the legitimate objective.
